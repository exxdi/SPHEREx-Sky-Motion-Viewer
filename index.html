Find moving objects

Automatic search: compares frames taken at the same wavelength on different observation dates, removes noise (median of all pairs + star-sized filter), lists what moved or changed brightness, and checks each one against Gaia.

● Unmatched — in no catalog, clean signal (worth reporting) · ● Known — Gaia, AllWISE or a known asteroid · ● Inconclusive — the reason is listed. Tag symbol: → moving · » fast (asteroid?) · ▲ brighter · ▼ fainter

    Overlays

    Averages each pixel with its neighbours at the size of a star: noise drops, real stars stay. Off = raw pixels.

    Red marks pixels the SPHEREx pipeline flagged — hot pixels, cosmic rays, saturation and more. A "moving object" sitting on red is usually an artifact.

    Circles mark stars already known to ESA's Gaia. Each arrow shows where that star moves in 10 years. A moving dot without a circle, or moving differently from its arrow, is worth reporting.

    Pink squares: infrared sources from NASA's WISE, with their W1/W2 brightness and motion. Brown dwarfs are bright here but faint for Gaia.

    How to spot something new

    Pluto was found by comparing two photos of the sky taken at different times. You do the same, frame by frame.

    A moving object

    1. The dot shifts while the stars around it stay put.
    2. It moves in a straight line, the same way between observation dates.
    3. In Clean difference mode it becomes a dark–bright pair.

    Turn on Gaia: if no known star there has that motion, you may have found something.

    A variable star

    1. The dot stays in place but gets brighter or fainter.
    2. Compare frames at the same wavelength (λ label).
    3. Click it to see whether the whole spectrum changed.

    Each SPHEREx frame is a different infrared color, so brightness also changes with λ.

    An artifact (not real)

    1. A sharp single-pixel spot with hard edges.
    2. Appears in one frame only.
    3. Check the flagged-pixel count for that frame.

    Cosmic rays and hot pixels are the most common false alarms.

    About the data

    SPHEREx

    NASA's SPHEREx maps the whole sky every six months in 102 near-infrared bands (0.75–5.0 µm) using six detector arrays, D1–D6. Every frame is taken at a slightly different wavelength.

    Images come live from the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (IRSA), which adds new data every week. Current releases: QR3 and DR1.

    This publication makes use of data products from the Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer (SPHEREx), which is a joint project of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and the California Institute of Technology, and is funded by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration.

    Gaia DR3

    Star positions and proper motions from ESA's Gaia Data Release 3 (positions measured for the year 2016.0), shifted to the date of each SPHEREx frame.
